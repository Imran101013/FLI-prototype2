Languages of Our Target Area: Northern Pakistan

The mountains of northern Pakistan are home to an extraordinary variety of languages. Explore the 29 languages FLI works with, where they are spoken, how they are related, and what has been done to document them.

bft
Perso-Arabic (Tibetan script until the 16th century)
بلتی
Baltistan (Skardu, Ghanche, Shigar, Kharmang), Gilgit-Baltistan
Tibeto-Burman • 400,000+

Balti is spoken in the Northern Areas (Gilgit-Baltistan) of Pakistan and adjoining parts of Ladakh, India. It is closely related to those languages found in Tibet. Though the population of Baltistan is a mixture of different ethnic groups, those whose ancestors originally came from Tibet form the principal ethnic group.

Vulnerable View profile

btv
No functional writing system yet (FLI documentation since 2021)
باتیری
Batera, southern Kohistan district, east bank of the Indus
Dardic (Indo-Aryan) • Up to 15,000

Belonging to the Kohistani group, the Bateri language is spoken in Batera, which is in the southern region of the Kohistan district along the east bank of the Indus River. According to the 2017 Population Census, the total recorded number of people in Batera Union Council was 18,000. Since Batera UC is also populated by people other than Bateri speakers, the figure of Bateri speakers is estimated to be up to 15,000 — a statistic provided by Bateri researcher Mr. Muhammad Baqi, who has long been collecting data about his native language. Some speakers of Bateri are also found in India, where more than 2,000 were reported in 2010.

Definitely Endangered View profile

bsk
Perso-Arabic (FLI alphabet book, 2023)
بروشسکی
Hunza, Nagar and Yasin valleys, Gilgit-Baltistan
Burushaski (Isolate) • About 100,000

Burushaski is a language isolate, as its relatedness to any language family of the world has yet to be established. It is spoken in the Northern Gilgit, Hunza-Nagar and Yasin areas. There are significant dialectal differences between the Yasin variety (Werchikwar) on the one hand, and the Hunza and Nagar varieties on the other. A small pocket of the Burushaski population is also found in Srinagar. The number of speakers is estimated at around 100,000.

Vulnerable View profile

clh
Unwritten / oral
چیلیسو
East bank of the Indus, Kohistan district (mostly Jalkot)
Dardic (Indo-Aryan) • A few thousand

According to the Sociolinguistic Survey of Northern Pakistan, conducted by the National Institute of Pakistan Studies, Quaid-e-Azam University, Islamabad and SIL International, the name Chilisso comes from Chiliss Khel, a clan name of those who speak this language. The speakers of Chilisso live in the midst of the majority Shina-speaking population.

Severely Endangered View profile

dml
Perso-Arabic (developed with FLI in 2016)
دمیلی
Damel valley, Arandu Tehsil, Lower Chitral
Dardic (Indo-Aryan) • About 6,000

Dameli is spoken in several isolated villages in a side valley called Damel, on the east side of the Chitral River, a few miles below Mirkhani in Arandu Tehsil, Chitral. Dameli has been substantially influenced by neighbouring languages and is threatened by the larger surrounding language communities as well as Urdu. Still, Dameli speakers use their language at home, with family and friends, in community and religious gatherings, and informally in schools.

Definitely Endangered View profile

dmk
Arabic script (documented); primarily spoken
دوماکی
Hunza and Nagar valleys, Gilgit-Baltistan
Indo-Aryan (Other) • 340 – a few hundred

Domaaki, also known locally as Dawoodi, is an endangered Indo-Aryan language spoken in the Hunza and Nagar valleys of Gilgit-Baltistan. It belongs to the Central Indo-Aryan group and is historically related to languages of the Indian Midlands, though it has been heavily influenced by neighbouring tongues such as Burushaski and Shina. Speaker estimates range from 340 to a few hundred individuals, making it one of the smallest linguistic communities in the region.

Critically Endangered View profile

gwt
Perso-Arabic (in written use for decades)
گوَرباتی
Arandu village, Chitral (and Kunar valley, Afghanistan)
Dardic (Indo-Aryan) • Under 7,000 in Pakistan

Gawarbati (gwt) is an Indo-European language spoken in the mountainous and partly inaccessible border region between northern Pakistan and northeastern Afghanistan. Locally known as Aranduyiwar in Pakistan, it belongs to the Dardic branch of the Indo-Aryan family and is closely related to Shumashti, Dameli and Grangeali. Estimates of its speaker population range between 9,000 and 75,000; fewer than 7,000 live in Pakistan.

Threatened View profile

gwc
Perso-Arabic (literate language)
گاؤری
Kalam (Swat) and Upper Dir valleys
Dardic (Indo-Aryan) • 1,200,000+ (as stated on the FLI page)

Gawri was referred to as Kalam Kohistani / Dir Kohistani for a long time. Gawri speakers generally refer to their language as Kohistani; it is spoken in the Swat and Upper Dir valleys of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. Gawri is a tonal language with five contrastive tones.

Threatened View profile

gju
Perso-Arabic (Nastaliq)
گوجری
Azad Kashmir, Hazara, Swat, Chitral and Gilgit-Baltistan
Indo-Aryan (Other) • 2,500,000+

Gojri belongs to the central group of the Indo-Aryan language family and is closely related to Mewati, a language spoken in north-eastern Rajasthan. Gojri is spoken in northern Afghanistan, northern Pakistan and the former state of Jammu and Kashmir. Most Gujars who settled in the plains have abandoned Gojri; those who continue speaking it include nomadic pastoralists, settled agriculturalists and semi-settled agriculturalists who practise seasonal transhumance.

Vulnerable View profile

gwf
Oral
گوورو
Mahrin village, east bank of the Indus, Kohistan
Dardic (Indo-Aryan) • 200 or fewer

Gowro, the language of the Gabar Khel clan, is spoken by a small number of people on the east bank of the Indus River in District Kohistan. The main village where Gowro is spoken is Mahrin, which has a majority Shina-speaking population. Mother-tongue speakers of Gowro are estimated at only 200 or fewer, while Shina speakers number more than 200,000 in the area. Gowro has 60 percent similarity with Bateri and the same with Chilisso.

Severely Endangered View profile

hnd
Perso-Arabic (Nastaliq)
ہندکو
Mansehra, Abbottabad, Haripur, Peshawar, Kohat (KP) and Attock
Indo-Aryan (Other) • About 5 million (2017 census)

Speakers of Hindko live primarily in Mansehra, Abbottabad, Haripur, Peshawar, Torghar, Battagram and Kohat in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, and in Attock in Punjab. According to 2017 census figures, around 5 million people speak Hindko as their mother tongue. Its predominance in rural areas is reflected in the fact that 2.8 percent of rural households report Hindko as their household language, compared with 1.5 percent of urban households.

Vulnerable View profile

mvy
Perso-Arabic
اباسِندھی کوہستانی
West bank of the Indus — Seo, Pattan, Jijal, Duber and Kandia
Dardic (Indo-Aryan) • About 400,000 (2017 census)

The name Kohistan means “the Land of Mountains”, and the region is known for its high and rugged mountains. Indus Kohistani is the major language of the west bank of the Indus River — an endangered Dardic language of the Indo-Aryan family, spoken by about 400,000 people according to the 2017 census.

Threatened View profile

kls
Latin & Perso-Arabic orthographies
کالاشہ
Rumbur, Bumburet and Birir valleys, Chitral
Dardic (Indo-Aryan) • 2,500 – 3,000

The Kalasha language is spoken in the Rumbur, Bumburet and Birir valleys of District Chitral, near the town of Ayun, south of Chitral; these represent the Northern dialect of Kalasha. The language most closely related to Kalasha is Khowar, which Kalasha people speak with varying degrees of proficiency.

Severely Endangered View profile

xka
Perso-Arabic (orthography developed with FLI, 2021–22)
کلکوٹی
Kalkot village, Dir Kohistan (Upper Dir)
Dardic (Indo-Aryan) • 6,500+

Kalkoti, a Dardic language, is spoken by more than 6,500 people in Kalkot village of Dir Kohistan. Although oral, the language is vigorous, as all age groups use it for communication. However, most of the community is bilingual in Pashto, the largest language of the province, and many linguists fear a language shift in future.

Definitely Endangered View profile

bsh / xvi
Perso-Arabic (orthography developed with FLI, 2021)
کتہ وری / کامویری
Shekhandeh (near Bumburet) and Gobor, Chitral
Nuristani • About 10,000 in Chitral

Kataviri (bsh), also called Kati and Shekhani, belongs to the Nuristani cluster. Locals in Chitral call it Bashgali-war, the language of those who came from Bashgal in Nuristan, Afghanistan. Its speakers migrated to Chitral in the late 1900s and settled in border villages such as Shekhandeh, adjacent to Bumburet valley, about 8 km from the Afghan border. Speakers estimate their number at around 10,000 in Chitral.

Definitely Endangered View profile

kas
Perso-Arabic (Nastaliq)
کٲشُر
Neelum valley, Forward Kahuta (Bagh) and Muzaffarabad, AJK
Dardic (Indo-Aryan) • 200,000+ in Azad Kashmir

Kashmiri belongs to the Northwestern Zone of the Indo-Aryan language family. The total population of Kashmiri mother-tongue speakers in Azad Kashmir is over 200,000, most of them in the Neelum valley, Forward Kahuta (district Bagh) and Muzaffarabad. Over six million people speak the language in Indian-administered Kashmir, where it is widely taught in schools.

Vulnerable View profile

khw
Perso-Arabic (first alphabet compiled in 1921)
کھوار
Chitral district and Ghizer (Gilgit-Baltistan)
Dardic (Indo-Aryan) • 400,000

Khowar speakers are the largest group in Chitral, although they do not share a common origin. People of other ancestry have over time left their languages in favour of Khowar, which is today the most widely understood and spoken language of the valley.

Vulnerable View profile

shd
Oral
کنڈل شاہی
Kundal Shahi village, Neelum valley, Azad Kashmir
Dardic (Indo-Aryan) • About 700

Kundal Shahi belongs to the North-Western group of the Indo-Aryan languages. It is closely related to Shina and is spoken as a native language by some 700 people in the predominantly Hindko-speaking village of Kundal Shahi in the Neelum Valley of Azad Kashmir — the only language exclusive to the Neelum Valley. The community’s ancestors migrated from the valley of Kashmir some three centuries ago.

Severely Endangered View profile

prs
Perso-Arabic (orthography workshop, June 2026)
مدکلشتی
Upper villages of Shishikuh valley, Lower Chitral
Iranian • 3,000 – 4,000

Madaklashti, a variety of Persian spoken in the upper villages of Shishikuh Valley in Lower Chitral, was recently taken up by FLI as part of its mission to preserve and strengthen linguistic heritage.

Definitely Endangered View profile

nlm
Unwritten — no writing system yet
مانکیالی
Danna village, Bandi Shungli, Mansehra district
Dardic (Indo-Aryan) • About 500

Mankiyali is an Indo-Aryan language of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, spoken by around 500 people in the isolated village of Danna in the Bandi Shungli union council of Mansehra District. The whole community is reported to be bilingual in Hindko. Mankiyali is not mutually intelligible with any surrounding language and has been influenced by Hindko and Gojri, particularly in the development of phonemic tone.

Critically Endangered View profile

oru
Perso-Arabic
اُرمڑی
Kaniguram, South Waziristan
Iranian • About 6,000 (Ethnologue 2013)

Ormuri is spoken in Kaniguram, a pocket within the Mahsud Pashtoon area of Waziristan. It is the ancestral language of the Burki tribe. Due to conflict in the area, much of the community has migrated to different parts of Pakistan.

Severely Endangered View profile

phr
Perso-Arabic (Nastaliq)
پہاڑی - پوٹھوہاری
Murree, the Galliat, Potohar plateau and Azad Kashmir
Indo-Aryan (Other) • 2.5 million (1981) + 1.5 million in AJK

Pahari is spoken in Murree Tehsil of Rawalpindi District. The Pahari area is often called the Galliat, a plural of a Hindko word meaning mountain pass. Another dialect, also called Pahari or Chibhali, extends into Azad Kashmir. Travelling from Murree to Abbottabad, the language changes from Pahari to Hindko between Ayubia and Nathiagali.

Vulnerable View profile

phl
Perso-Arabic (recently developed written form)
پالُولا
Ashret and Biori valleys, Lower Chitral
Dardic (Indo-Aryan) • 10,000

Whether you use the old winding road over the Lowari Pass or drive through the new tunnel, the first communities you meet entering Chitral by road are the Palula. They inhabit the Ashret and Biori valleys and are traditionally considered the gatekeepers of Chitral. Palula is related to Shina and is widely used between Lowari and the town of Drosh. In recent years a written form has been developed and is used in a small but growing body of local literature and educational materials.

Definitely Endangered View profile

pus
Perso-Arabic (Naskh)
پښتو
Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, northern Balochistan and the tribal districts
Iranian • About 25 million in Pakistan

In Pakistan, Pashto is spoken across Khyber Pakhtunkhwa — including Peshawar, Hazara, Kohat, Bannu and Dera Ismail Khan, Swat, Buner and Bajaur — as well as northern Balochistan (Quetta-Pishin, Loralai, Zhob and Sibi) and the border areas of Mianwali and Attock. The whole tribal area between Pakistan and Afghanistan is Pashto territory, and Karachi has a large Pashto-speaking population.

Vulnerable View profile

scl
Perso-Arabic (Nastaliq)
شینا
Gilgit, Diamer, Astore and Ghizer (Gilgit-Baltistan) and Kohistan
Dardic (Indo-Aryan) • 650,000

Gilgiti Shina is spoken in the valley of the Gilgit River and almost the entire area of its tributaries. The largest number of speakers live around Gilgit town, the centre of trade, government and education for Gilgit-Baltistan. Radio Pakistan Gilgit broadcasts several hours of programming each day in Shina, using the Gilgit dialect.

Vulnerable View profile

trw
Perso-Arabic
توروالی
Bahrain and Chail, Swat district
Dardic (Indo-Aryan) • 120,000 – 130,000

A Dardic language of the Indo-Aryan family, Torwali is spoken in the Bahrain and Chail areas of District Swat. By some estimates the Torwali people number between 120,000 and 130,000, possibly half of them in the Swat heartland.

Threatened View profile

ush
Perso-Arabic (orthography developed with FLI)
اوشوجو
Twelve villages around Bishigram and Madyan, Swat
Dardic (Indo-Aryan) • About 9,000

Ushojo, a highly endangered language, is spoken in twelve villages of the Swat valley, including Bishigram, Shepiza, Kas, Derai, Nalkot, Karial, Sore, Tangai Banda, Kappal Banda, Moghul Mar, Tukai and Danda, and by speakers in Madyan. Speakers estimate their number at about nine thousand; a 1992 survey counted two to three thousand.

Critically Endangered View profile

wbl
Latin & Perso-Arabic orthographies
وخی
Gojal (Upper Hunza), Ishkoman, Yasin and Yarkhun (Chitral)
Iranian • 7,500 – 10,000

Wakhi is spoken in the upper portions of four of the northernmost valleys of Pakistan: Hunza (Gojal), Ishkoman, Yasin and Yarkhun. Gojal, the part of the Hunza valley from Gulmit northwards, has the largest Wakhi population. The total number of Wakhi speakers is estimated at between 7,500 and 10,000.

Definitely Endangered View profile

ydg
Perso-Arabic (first publications 2016)
یدغہ
Upper Lutkoh valley, Lower Chitral
Iranian • About 6,000

Yadgha, also spelled Yidgha, is an endangered Eastern Iranian language of the Pamir group. It is spoken by around 6,000 people in the Upper Lutkoh valley of Lower Chitral and is closely related to Munji, spoken across the border in Afghanistan. The community lives in a handful of villages stretching west from Garam Chashma toward the Dorah Pass.
